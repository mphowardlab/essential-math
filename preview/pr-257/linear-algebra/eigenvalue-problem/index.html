3.6 Eigenvalue problem

For an n x n square matrix A, we seek a scalar λ\lambdaλ and vector x such that:

Ax=λx\begin{align} \vv{A} \vv{x} = \lambda \vv{x} \end{align}Ax=λx​​
(1)

We want a nontrivial solution (x≠0\vv{x} \ne \vv{0}x=0). This pair represents a vector x that, when multiplied into A, does not change its direction. However, it may adopt a new magnitude λ\lambdaλ. Borrowing from a German word for “own”, we call x an eigenvector and λ\lambdaλ an eigenvalue of A.

To find the eigenvalues of A, rearrange:

Ax−λx=0(A−λI)x=0\begin{align} \vv{A} \vv{x} - \lambda \vv{x} &= \vv{0} \\ (\vv{A} - \lambda \vv{I}) \vv{x} &= \vv{0} \end{align}Ax−λx(A−λI)x​=0=0​​
(2)

If A−λI\vv{A} - \lambda \vv{I}A−λI is invertible, we have only x=0\vv{x} = \vv{0}x=0 as a solution. Hence, we require this matrix to be singular! This occurs when its determinant is zero:

∣A−λI∣=0\begin{align} |\vv{A}- \lambda \vv{I}| = 0 \end{align}∣A−λI∣=0​​
(3)

This equation creates a characeristic polynomial of degree n for λ\lambdaλ that can be solved. Then, the eigenvector x that corresponds to each root λ\lambdaλ can be determined. For example, to find the eigenvalues of:

A=[−522−2]\vv{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -5 & 2\\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}A=[−52​2−2​]
(4)

First compute the determinant:

∣A−λI∣=∣−5−λ22−2−λ∣=(−5−λ)(−2−λ)−2⋅2=λ2+7λ+6=(λ+1)(λ+6)=0\begin{align} |\vv{A} -\lambda \vv{I}| &= \begin{vmatrix} -5-\lambda & 2 \\ 2 & -2-\lambda \end{vmatrix} \\ &= (-5 -\lambda)(-2-\lambda) - 2 \cdot 2 \\ &= \lambda^2 +7\lambda + 6 \\ &= (\lambda +1)(\lambda +6) = 0 \end{align}∣A−λI∣​=∣∣​−5−λ2​2−2−λ​∣∣​=(−5−λ)(−2−λ)−2⋅2=λ2+7λ+6=(λ+1)(λ+6)=0​​
(5)

Hence, the eigenvalues of A are λ1=−1\lambda_1 = -1λ1​=−1 and λ2=−6\lambda_2 = -6λ2​=−6. Note that the particular ordering of the eigenvalues is not important, and we are only labeling them to make it convenient to refer to a particular eigenvalue later.

Next, we seek the eigenvector x1\vv{x}_1x1​ that corresponds to λ1\lambda_1λ1​. This vector must solve the system:

(A−λ1I)x1=0[−422−1]x1=0\begin{align} (\vv{A} - \lambda_1 \vv{I}) \vv{x}_1 &= \vv{0} \\ \begin{bmatrix} -4 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \vv{x}_1 &= \vv{0} \end{align}(A−λ1​I)x1​[−42​2−1​]x1​​=0=0​​
(6)

We can solve for x1\vv{x}_1x1​ using row reduction. Since the last column of the augmented matrix would be only zeros, it is not necessary to include it, and we row reduce only the matrix itself:

[−422−1]R1+R2/2→[−4200]÷−2R2→[1−1/200]\begin{bmatrix} -4 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{matrix}\vphantom{R_1} \\ +R_2/2 \end{matrix} \to \begin{bmatrix} -4 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{matrix} \div -2 \\ \vphantom{R_2} \end{matrix} \to \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1/2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}[−42​2−1​]R1​+R2​/2​→[−40​20​]÷−2R2​​→[10​−1/20​]
(7)

Note that this reduced matrix is equivalent to x1=x2/2x_1 = x_2/2x1​=x2​/2 (using x to represent elements of x1\vv{x}_1x1​ for convenience) with x2x_2x2​ being free. This means that the eigenvector is not unique, which makes sense both from the original equation we are trying to solve and us forcing the matrix to be singular! Equivalently, eigenvectors can be scaled up or down by an arbitrary nonzero constant. We may then choose x2x_2x2​ so that the eigenvector has either nice values (like like integers) or a nice norm (1 is conventional). For example, choosing x2=2x_2 = 2x2​=2 gives:

x1=[12]\vv{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}x1​=[12​]
(8)
Tip

For a 2x2 matrix, if the eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ has been found correctly, one row of A−λI\vv{A}-\lambda\vv{I}A−λI will always be a multiple of the other. This means the eigenvector can be determined using only one row. Moreover, the solution for x can be obtained by swapping the entries in a row and changing the sign of one of them! For example, we had

[−422−1]\begin{bmatrix} -4 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}[−42​2−1​]
(9)

so using the second row, we find:

x1=[−(−1)2]=[12]\vv{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}-(-1) \\ 2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}x1​=[−(−1)2​]=[12​]
(10)

However, we could also have negated the other entry:

x1=[−1−2]\vv{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}-1\\ -2\end{bmatrix}x1​=[−1−2​]
(11)

or used the first row:

x1=[24]\vv{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}x1​=[24​]
(12)

Both of these are also valid eigenvectors because they are multiples of the one we found already.

Let’s use the same strategy to get the eigenvector for λ2=−6\lambda_2 = -6λ2​=−6. We’ll jump straight to the matrix we need:

(A−λ2I)=[1224]→x2=[2−1]\begin{align} (\vv{A} - \lambda_2 \vv{I}) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \to \vv{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \end{align}(A−λ2​I)=[12​24​]→x2​=[2−1​]​​
(13)

using the swapping trick on the first row.

Multiple eigenvalues¶

An n x n matrix has n eigenvalues, but they may not be distinct! For example, consider the matrix:

A=[−22−321−6−1−20]\vv{A} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 2 & -3 \\ 2 & 1 & -6 \\ -1 & -2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}A=⎣⎡​−22−1​21−2​−3−60​⎦⎤​
(14)

We need to evaluate the determinant:

∣A−λI∣=∣−2−λ2−321−λ−6−1−2−λ∣=−1∣2−31−λ−6∣−(−2)∣−2−λ−32−6∣−λ∣−2−λ221−λ∣=−[−12+3(1−λ)]+2[6(2+λ)+6]−λ[(λ+2)(λ−1)−4]=−λ3−λ2+21λ+45=0\begin{align} |\vv{A} - \lambda \vv{I}| &= \begin{vmatrix} -2-\lambda & 2 & -3 \\ 2 & 1-\lambda & -6 \\ -1 & -2 & -\lambda \end{vmatrix} \\ &= -1 \begin{vmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ 1-\lambda & -6 \end{vmatrix} -(-2) \begin{vmatrix} -2-\lambda & -3 \\ 2 & -6 \end{vmatrix} -\lambda \begin{vmatrix} -2-\lambda & 2 \\ 2 & 1-\lambda \end{vmatrix} \\ &= -[-12+3(1-\lambda)]+2[6(2+\lambda)+6]-\lambda[(\lambda+2)(\lambda-1)-4] \\ &= -\lambda^3-\lambda^2+21\lambda+45 = 0 \end{align}∣A−λI∣​=∣∣​−2−λ2−1​21−λ−2​−3−6−λ​∣∣​=−1∣∣​21−λ​−3−6​∣∣​−(−2)∣∣​−2−λ2​−3−6​∣∣​−λ∣∣​−2−λ2​21−λ​∣∣​=−[−12+3(1−λ)]+2[6(2+λ)+6]−λ[(λ+2)(λ−1)−4]=−λ3−λ2+21λ+45=0​​
(15)

The roots of this cubic polynomial are λ1=5\lambda_1 = 5λ1​=5 and λ2=λ3=−3\lambda_2 = \lambda_3 = -3λ2​=λ3​=−3. The eigenvector for λ1=5\lambda_1 = 5λ1​=5 is obtained from

A−λ1I=[−72−32−4−6−1−2−3]→→[101012000]\vv{A} - \lambda_1 \vv{I} = \begin{bmatrix} -7 & 2 & -3 \\ 2 & -4 & -6 \\ -1 & -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix} \to\to \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}A−λ1​I=⎣⎡​−72−1​2−4−2​−3−6−3​⎦⎤​→→⎣⎡​100​010​120​⎦⎤​
(16)

This matrix is equivalent to the system of equations:

x1+x3=0x2+2x3=0\begin{align} x_1 + x_3 &= 0 \\ x_2 + 2 x_3 &= 0 \end{align}x1​+x3​x2​+2x3​​=0=0​​
(17)

with x3x_3x3​ free. Choosing x3=−1x_3 = -1x3​=−1 gives

x1=[12−1]\vv{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}x1​=⎣⎡​12−1​⎦⎤​
(18)

The eigenvectors for λ2=λ3=−3\lambda_2 = \lambda_3 = -3λ2​=λ3​=−3 are obtained from

A−λ2I=[12−324−6−1−23]→→[12−3000000]\vv{A} - \lambda_2 \vv{I} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -3 \\ 2 & 4 & -6 \\ -1 & -2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \to\to \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}A−λ2​I=⎣⎡​12−1​24−2​−3−63​⎦⎤​→→⎣⎡​100​200​−300​⎦⎤​
(19)

This matrix is equivalent to

x1+2x2−3x3=0x_1 + 2x_2 - 3x_3 = 0x1​+2x2​−3x3​=0
(20)

with x2x_2x2​ and x3x_3x3​ free. Choosing x2=1x_2 = 1x2​=1 and x3=0x_3 = 0x3​=0 gives one eigenvector

x2=[−210]\vv{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}x2​=⎣⎡​−210​⎦⎤​
(21)

while choosing an independent combination x2=0x_2 = 0x2​=0 and x3=1x_3 = 1x3​=1 gives

x3=[301]\vv{x}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}x3​=⎣⎡​301​⎦⎤​
(22)

Complex eigenvalues¶

Matrices may have complex eigenvaules. They always come in conjugate pairs! For example, for the matrix:

A=[01−10]\vv{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}A=[0−1​10​]
(23)

We evaluate the determinant:

∣A−λI∣=∣−λ1−1λ∣=λ2+1=0λ1,2=±i\begin{align} |\vv{A} - \lambda \vv{I}| &= \begin{vmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ -1 & \lambda \end{vmatrix} \\ &= \lambda^2 + 1 = 0 \\ \lambda_{1,2} = \pm i \end{align}∣A−λI∣λ1,2​=±i​=∣∣​−λ−1​1λ​∣∣​=λ2+1=0​​
(24)

For λ1=i\lambda_1 = iλ1​=i:

A−λ1I=[−i1−1i]→[−i100]\vv{A} - \lambda_1 \vv{I} = \begin{bmatrix} -i & 1 \\ -1 & i \end{bmatrix} \to \begin{bmatrix} -i & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}A−λ1​I=[−i−1​1i​]→[−i0​10​]
(25)

This matrix is equivalent to

−ix1+x2=0-ix_1 + x_2 = 0−ix1​+x2​=0
(26)

with x2x_2x2​ free. Choosing x2=ix_2 = ix2​=i gives

x1=[1i]\vv{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i\end{bmatrix}x1​=[1i​]
(27)

For λ2=−i\lambda_2 = -iλ2​=−i:

A−λ1I=[i1−1i]→[i100]\vv{A} - \lambda_1 \vv{I} = \begin{bmatrix} i & 1 \\ -1 & i \end{bmatrix} \to \begin{bmatrix} i & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}A−λ1​I=[i−1​1i​]→[i0​10​]
(28)

This matrix is equivalent to

ix1+x2=0i x_1 + x_2 = 0ix1​+x2​=0
(29)

with x2x_2x2​ free. Choosing x2=−ix_2 = -ix2​=−i gives

x2=[1−i]\vv{x}_2 =\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix}x2​=[1−i​]
(30)

Useful tricks¶

  • The eigenvaules of upper triangular matrices are the values on the diagonal. For example, the matrix

    [145026003]\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 5 \\ 0 & 2 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}⎣⎡​100​420​563​⎦⎤​
    (31)

    has λ1=1\lambda_1 = 1λ1​=1, λ2=2\lambda_2 = 2λ2​=2, and λ3=3\lambda_3 = 3λ3​=3 as eigenvalues.

  • The transpose AT\vv{A}^{\rm T}AT has the same eigenvalues as A\vv{A}A. For example, the matrix:

    [100420563]\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 4 & 2 & 0 \\ 5 & 6 & 3 \end{bmatrix}⎣⎡​145​026​003​⎦⎤​
    (32)

    also has λ1=1\lambda_1 = 1λ1​=1, λ2=2\lambda_2 = 2λ2​=2, and λ3=3\lambda_3 = 3λ3​=3 as eigenvalues because it is the transpose of the matrix above.

    Note that this means that the eigenvalues of lower triangular matrices are also the values on the diagonal!

  • If A is symmetric (AT=A\vv{A}^{\rm T} = \vv{A}AT=A), its eigenvalues are real.

  • If A is skew-symmetric (AT=−A\vv{A}^{\rm T} = -\vv{A}AT=−A), its eigenvalues are purely imaginary.

Skill builder problems¶

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors

  1. For:

    A=[420−4]\vv{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}A=[40​2−4​]
    (33)
    Since **A** is upper triangular, the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries, $\lambda_1 = 4$ and $\lambda_2 = -4$. Then, use these eigenvalues to find the eigenvectors that solve $(\vv{A}-\lambda \vv{I})\vv{x} = \vv{0}$. For $\lambda_1 = 4$, \begin{equation} \vv{A}-\lambda_1\vv{I} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & -8 \end{bmatrix} \to \vv{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation} For $\lambda_2 = -4$, \begin{equation} \vv{A}-\lambda_2\vv{I} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \to \vv{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation}

  2. For:

    A=[5−29−6]\vv{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ 9 & -6 \end{bmatrix}A=[59​−2−6​]
    (34)
    First, find the eigenvalues: \begin{align} |\vv{A}-\lambda \vv{I}| &= \begin{vmatrix} 5 - \lambda & -2 \\ 9 & -6 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} \\ &= (\lambda - 5)(\lambda + 6) + 18 \\ &= \lambda^2 + \lambda - 12 \\ &= (\lambda - 3)(\lambda + 4) = 0 \end{align} So, $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = -4$. For $\lambda_1 = 4$, \begin{equation} \vv{A}-\lambda_1\vv{I} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ 9 & -9 \end{bmatrix} \to \vv{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation} For $\lambda_2 = -4$, \begin{equation} \vv{A}-\lambda_2\vv{I} = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & -2 \\ 9 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \to \vv{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation}

  3. For:

    A=[62−2250−207]\vv{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 & -2 \\ 2 & 5 & 0 \\ -2 & 0 & 7 \end{bmatrix}A=⎣⎡​62−2​250​−207​⎦⎤​
    (35)
    First, find the eigenvalues: \begin{align} |\vv{A}-\lambda \vv{I}| &= \begin{vmatrix} 6 - \lambda & 2 & -2 \\ 2 & 5 - \lambda & 0 \\ -2 & 0 & 7 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} \\ &= -2 \begin{vmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 7 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} + (5 - \lambda) \begin{vmatrix} 6 - \lambda & -2 \\ -2 & 7 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} \\ &= -2 \cdot 2(7 - \lambda) + (5 - \lambda)[(\lambda - 6)(\lambda - 7) - 4] \\ &= -\lambda^3 + 18\lambda^2 - 99\lambda + 162 = 0 \end{align} Solving for these roots numerically gives $\lambda_1 = 9$, $\lambda_2 = 6$, and $\lambda_3 = 3$. For $\lambda_1 = 9$, using row reduction gives \begin{equation} \vv{A}-\lambda_1\vv{I} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 2 & -2 \\ 2 & -4 & 0 \\ -2 & 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \to\to \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation} Choosing $x_3 = 2$ as the free variable gives \begin{equation} \vv{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation} For $\lambda_2 = 6$, using row reduction gives \begin{equation} \vv{A}-\lambda_2\vv{I} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -2 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \to\to \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation} Taking $x_3 = 2$ as the free v ariable gives: \begin{equation} \vv{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation} For $\lambda_3 = 3$, using row reduction gives \begin{equation} \vv{A}-\lambda_3\vv{I} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 & -2 \\ 2 & 2 & 0 \\ -2 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \to\to \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation} Choosing $x_3 = 1$ as the free variable gives: \begin{equation} \vv{x}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation}
